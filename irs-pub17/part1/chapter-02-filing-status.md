## Introduction

Your filing status is used to determine your:
- Filing requirements
- Standard deduction
- Eligibility for certain credits
- Tax rates

You may be able to choose between more than one filing status. **Choose the one that results in the lowest tax.**

### The Five Filing Statuses

1. Single
2. Married Filing Jointly
3. Married Filing Separately
4. Head of Household
5. Qualifying Surviving Spouse

---

## Single

### Requirements

Your filing status is **Single** if on the last day of the year you are:
- Unmarried, **or**
- Legally separated from your spouse under a divorce or separate maintenance decree, **or**
- Widowed before January 1, 2025, and didn't remarry in 2025

### Not Single If...

You can't use Single status if:
- You were married on December 31, 2025
- You are an unmarried parent who qualifies for Head of Household status
- You are a surviving spouse who qualifies for Qualifying Surviving Spouse status

> **Tip:** If you qualify for Head of Household, you'll usually pay less tax than if you file as Single.

## Married Filing Jointly

### Requirements

You can file a joint return if you are married and both you and your spouse agree to file a joint return.

**You are considered married for the whole year if on the last day of the year:**
- You and your spouse are married and living together
- You are living together in a common law marriage recognized in the state where you now live or in the state where the common law marriage began
- You are married and living apart but not legally separated under a decree of divorce or separate maintenance
- You are separated under an interlocutory (not final) decree of divorce

### Same-Sex Couples

Same-sex couples who are legally married are treated as married for federal tax purposes.

### Advantages of Joint Filing

Usually, you pay less tax if you file jointly because:
- Your tax rates may be lower
- You can claim certain credits only on a joint return
- Your standard deduction is higher ($31,500 for 2025)

### Joint Responsibility

If you file a joint return, **both spouses are jointly and individually responsible** for the tax and any interest or penalty due on the return. This means:
- If one spouse doesn't pay, the other may have to
- This applies even if you later divorce

**Innocent spouse relief** may be available if your spouse understated tax without your knowledge. See [Innocent Spouse Relief](#innocent-spouse-relief) below.

### Spouse Died During the Year

If your spouse died during 2025 and you didn't remarry in 2025, you can file a joint return for 2025 with your deceased spouse.

## Married Filing Separately

### When To Consider Filing Separately

You may want to file separately if:
- You want to be responsible only for your own tax
- You believe your spouse is not reporting all income
- You are separated and can't or won't file jointly
- Filing separately results in less tax (rare but possible)

### Disadvantages of Filing Separately

If you file separately, you generally:
- Have higher tax rates
- Can't take the standard deduction if your spouse itemizes
- Can't claim certain credits:
  - Earned Income Credit
  - Child and Dependent Care Credit (usually)
  - American Opportunity Credit (usually)
  - Lifetime Learning Credit (usually)
- Have lower limits for some deductions
- Can't deduct student loan interest
- Must include more Social Security benefits as income

### Standard Deduction If Filing Separately

Your standard deduction is **$15,750** for 2025.

However, if your spouse itemizes deductions, **you cannot take the standard deduction**. You must either itemize or enter zero for your standard deduction.

### Community Property States

If you live in a community property state and file separately, special rules apply. Community property states are:
- Arizona
- California
- Idaho
- Louisiana
- Nevada
- New Mexico
- Texas
- Washington
- Wisconsin

See Pub. 555, Community Property.

## Head of Household

### Requirements

You may file as Head of Household if you meet ALL of the following:

1. **Unmarried Test:** You were unmarried or "considered unmarried" on the last day of the year.

2. **Paid More Than Half the Cost of Keeping Up a Home:** You paid more than half the cost of keeping up a home for the year.

3. **Qualifying Person:** A qualifying person lived with you in your home for more than half the year (except temporary absences).

### Considered Unmarried

You are "considered unmarried" and can file as Head of Household if ALL of the following apply:
- You file a separate return
- You paid more than half the cost of keeping up your home for the tax year
- Your spouse didn't live in your home during the last 6 months of the tax year (temporary absences don't count)
- Your home was the main home of your child, stepchild, or foster child for more than half the year
- You can claim the child as a dependent, or could claim the child except that the noncustodial parent claims the child

### Qualifying Person

A qualifying person is:

| Qualifying Person | Must Live With You |
|-------------------|-------------------|
| Qualifying child (son, daughter, stepchild, foster child, sibling, or descendant) | More than half the year* |
| Qualifying relative who is your parent | Does NOT need to live with you** |
| Qualifying relative (other than parent) | Entire year |

*Exceptions for temporary absences (school, illness, business, military, etc.)

**You must pay more than half the cost of keeping up the parent's home.

### Cost of Keeping Up a Home

**Includes:**
- Rent or mortgage interest
- Property taxes
- Home insurance
- Repairs
- Utilities
- Food eaten in the home

**Does NOT include:**
- Clothing
- Education
- Medical expenses
- Vacations
- Life insurance
- Transportation

### Standard Deduction

The standard deduction for Head of Household is **$23,625** for 2025.

## Qualifying Surviving Spouse

### Requirements

You may file as a Qualifying Surviving Spouse if ALL of the following apply:

1. **Spouse Died:** Your spouse died in 2023 or 2024, and you didn't remarry in 2025.

2. **Qualifying Child:** You have a child, stepchild, or foster child who qualifies as your dependent (or would qualify except that the noncustodial parent claims the child).

3. **Paid More Than Half:** You paid more than half the cost of keeping up a home that was the main home for you and this child for all of 2025.

4. **Joint Return:** You could have filed a joint return with your spouse the year he or she died.

### How Long You Can Use This Status

| Year Spouse Died | Years You Can Use QSS Status |
|------------------|------------------------------|
| 2024 | 2025 only |
| 2023 | 2024 and 2025 |

After the 2-year period ends, you may qualify for Head of Household status.

### Advantages

Filing as Qualifying Surviving Spouse allows you to use:
- Joint return tax rates
- The highest standard deduction ($31,500 for 2025)

## Filing Status Decision Tree

```
Are you married on December 31?
│
├── YES → Do you want to file jointly?
│         │
│         ├── YES → Married Filing Jointly
│         │
│         └── NO → Married Filing Separately
│                  (Consider if separate is beneficial)
│
└── NO → Did your spouse die in 2023 or 2024?
         │
         ├── YES → Do you have a qualifying child?
         │         │
         │         ├── YES → Qualifying Surviving Spouse
         │         │
         │         └── NO → Single (or HOH if qualifies)
         │
         └── NO → Do you qualify for Head of Household?
                  │
                  ├── YES → Head of Household
                  │
                  └── NO → Single
```

## Innocent Spouse Relief

If you filed a joint return and believe you should not be held responsible for your spouse's (or former spouse's) tax, you may be able to get relief.

### Types of Relief

1. **Innocent Spouse Relief:** You didn't know about the understatement of tax
2. **Separation of Liability:** You're no longer married or are legally separated
3. **Equitable Relief:** You don't qualify for the other types but it would be unfair to hold you liable

### How To Apply

File **Form 8857**, Request for Innocent Spouse Relief.

**Time Limit:** Generally, you must file Form 8857 no later than 2 years after the date the IRS first attempted to collect the tax from you.
